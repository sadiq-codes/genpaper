# Literature Review Context

A literature review SYNTHESIZES existing research—it does NOT involve original data collection. Your job is to survey, analyze, and critically evaluate what scholars have already written.

---

## Literature Review Types

Based on the purpose and scope, determine which type best matches:

**How to identify the type:**
- Survey of a field as a standalone paper → Type A
- Foundation for your own research (thesis/dissertation chapter) → Type B
- Rigorous, replicable synthesis with explicit criteria → Type C
- Broad mapping of a new or complex area → Type D

### Type A: Standalone Literature Review
A complete paper that surveys and synthesizes scholarship on a topic.
- **Purpose:** Map the current state of knowledge, identify patterns and gaps
- **Audience:** Readers wanting a comprehensive overview of a field
- **Output:** Synthesis of existing research without original empirical contribution

### Type B: Background/Context Chapter
A section of a larger work (thesis, dissertation) establishing scholarly foundation.
- **Purpose:** Situate YOUR research within existing scholarship
- **Audience:** Committee members evaluating your scholarly preparation
- **Output:** Foundation that justifies your research questions and methodology

### Type C: Systematic Review
A rigorous, methodical review with explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria.
- **Purpose:** Comprehensive, replicable synthesis of evidence on a specific question
- **Audience:** Scholars who need exhaustive, reproducible coverage
- **Output:** Transparent methodology + comprehensive synthesis of all relevant evidence

### Type D: Scoping Review
A broad exploration mapping key concepts, sources, and evidence types.
- **Purpose:** Clarify concepts, identify scope of evidence, inform future research
- **Audience:** Researchers exploring a new or evolving area
- **Output:** Map of concepts, evidence types, and gaps in an emerging field

---

## Critical Distinctions

### What "Methodology" Means for Literature Reviews

"Methodology" for a literature review means the **LITERATURE SEARCH methodology**:
- Databases searched (Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, etc.)
- Keywords and search strings used
- Date range covered
- Inclusion/exclusion criteria applied
- Number of papers screened and selected

"Methodology" does **NOT** mean empirical research methods:
- NO descriptions of data collection (surveys, interviews, experiments)
- NO sample sizes or participant recruitment
- NO statistical analysis of original data

### Section Guidelines
- Literature reviews should NOT have a "Results" section presenting original findings
- Use sections like "Findings," "Thematic Analysis," or "Synthesis" instead
- Emphasize: synthesis across sources, critical analysis, gap identification

## Finding Your Focus

A literature review is organized around **IDEAS**, not sources.

**Key Principle:** You are not writing an annotated bibliography where each source gets its own paragraph. You are constructing an argument or narrative about the state of knowledge.

### Identify Your Central Theme
Ask yourself:
- What question am I answering about this body of literature?
- What story does this literature tell?
- What patterns, debates, or gaps emerge?

Your focus might be:
- A **research question** the literature addresses
- A **debate** between competing perspectives
- A **problem** the literature reveals
- An **evolution** of understanding over time

**Anti-pattern:** Don't organize as "Smith says X, Jones says Y, Brown says Z." Instead, organize around themes with sources as supporting evidence.

---

## Introduction Framework

Your introduction should establish:

### Focus Statement
What is the central topic or question this review addresses? Be specific.

### Scope
What's included and excluded? Define boundaries:
- Time period covered
- Geographic or population focus
- Disciplinary boundaries
- Types of sources included

### Purpose
Why does this review matter? What will readers gain?
- For Type A: Why is this synthesis needed now?
- For Type B: How does this prepare for your research?
- For Type C/D: What specific question drives the search?

### Organizational Preview
How is this review structured? What approach (thematic, chronological, etc.) and why?

---

## Organizational Approaches

Choose the approach that best illuminates your topic (or combine approaches):

### Thematic
Organize by recurring themes or concepts.
- **Best when:** Topic has multiple distinct aspects or sub-areas
- **Example:** "Barriers to healthcare" → policy barriers, economic barriers, cultural barriers

### Chronological
Trace the development of ideas over time.
- **Best when:** Understanding has evolved significantly; showing change matters
- **Example:** "Distance learning" → correspondence courses → video lectures → MOOCs → AI tutors

### Methodological
Compare findings from different research approaches.
- **Best when:** Different methods yield different insights or conflicting results
- **Example:** "Employee motivation" → survey studies vs. experimental studies vs. qualitative interviews

### Theoretical
Organize by competing theories or frameworks.
- **Best when:** Multiple theoretical perspectives explain the phenomenon
- **Example:** "Organizational change" → planned change theory vs. emergent change vs. complexity theory

For complex topics, COMBINE approaches (e.g., thematic overall with chronological development within each theme).

---

## Pivotal Publications

Identify and highlight LANDMARK STUDIES that shaped the field:
- Foundational works that established key concepts
- Studies that challenged assumptions and redirected research
- Methodological innovations that changed how the topic is studied
- Highly influential works that subsequent studies build upon

When discussing pivotal works, explain their **INFLUENCE**—not just what they found, but how they changed the field.

## Body Structure

### Within Each Section:
- Open with your analytical point (your voice)
- Support with evidence from multiple sources
- Synthesize—show patterns across studies
- Close with your interpretation or transition

### Between Sections:
- Use transitions showing how themes connect
- Build toward your overall argument or gap identification
- Maintain logical flow from one theme to the next

---

## Maintaining Your Voice

Your voice as analyst should remain **front and center**. You are not merely reporting—you are interpreting, synthesizing, and evaluating.

### Balance Reporting and Analysis
- **Weak:** "Smith (2020) found X. Jones (2021) found Y."
- **Strong:** "While early studies focused on X [1][2], recent work has shifted toward Y [3][4], suggesting a growing recognition that..."

### Strategies for Maintaining Voice:
1. **Start and end paragraphs with your analysis**—sources support your points
2. **Use reporting verbs strategically**—"argues," "demonstrates," "suggests," "challenges" show interpretation
3. **Make connections explicit**—"This contradicts..." "Building on this foundation..."
4. **Offer critical assessment**—"While compelling, this approach overlooks..."

### Anti-patterns:
- Disappearing behind sources (no analytical statements)
- Excessive direct quoting (paraphrase and synthesize instead)
- Source-by-source organization (organize by theme)
- Letting one source dominate multiple paragraphs

---

## Critical Analysis Requirements

A literature review MUST include critical analysis, not just summaries.

### Synthesis Patterns

❌ **Avoid (Descriptive listing):**
> "[Author A] found X. [Author B] found Y. [Author C] found Z."

✅ **Use (Integrated synthesis):**
> "A consistent pattern emerges across recent studies: researchers examining [topic] have found [finding] [1][2][3]. However, findings diverge regarding [aspect], with [some studies] suggesting [one finding] [4] while [other studies] reveal [different finding] [5]. This [type of divide] may explain the apparent contradiction..."

### Critical Evaluation Framework

For major findings or claims, evaluate:

**Methodological Quality:**
- Sample size adequacy
- Design appropriateness
- Potential biases or confounds
- Measurement validity

**Generalizability:**
- Context limitations (geographic, temporal, demographic)
- Population constraints
- Replication status

**Contradictions:**
- Studies with conflicting findings
- Possible explanations for discrepancies
- Unresolved debates

---

## Conclusion

Your conclusion should answer: **"What does this literature tell us, and what remains unknown?"**

### Required Elements:

**Synthesis of Key Findings:**
What are the main conclusions from this body of literature? What consensus exists?

**Gap Statement (REQUIRED):**
What questions remain unanswered? What has been overlooked? Be specific—not "more research is needed."

**Future Directions:**
- For Type A: How might future research address the gaps?
- For Type B: How does YOUR research address the gap?

**Significance:**
Why does this state of knowledge matter for theory, practice, or policy?

### Anti-patterns:
- Introducing new sources not discussed in the body
- Simply restating section headings
- Vague gap statements ("more research is needed")
- Overstating conclusions beyond what literature supports

---

## When to Cite

Since a literature review synthesizes existing research, MOST claims require citations.

**ALWAYS cite:**
- Statistics, findings, theories, methods from other authors
- Specific claims, definitions, frameworks
- When comparing what different authors found

**NO citation needed:**
- Your own synthesis, analysis, or conclusions drawn from cited work
- Widely accepted common knowledge in the field

Most paragraphs should contain multiple citations. Only your analytical observations connecting sources don't require citations.

---

## Table Requirements

When synthesizing multiple studies, USE TABLES to organize complex information.

### Recommended Tables (include at least ONE):

**Summary of Reviewed Studies**
| Author (Year) | Sample/Context | Method | Key Findings |

**Comparison of Approaches**
| Approach | Advantages | Limitations | Supporting Studies |

**Contradictory Findings**
| Finding | Studies Supporting | Studies Contradicting | Possible Explanation |

### Table Guidelines:
- Include citation markers in Author or Studies columns
- Keep cell content concise
- Add interpretive paragraph below each table
- Tables complement prose—don't replace synthesis

---

## Source Diversity

Literature reviews MUST demonstrate comprehensive coverage:
- Cite **70-100%** of available relevant sources
- Each thematic section: draw from **multiple different sources**
- Avoid over-reliance on any single source (max 4-5 citations per source)
- Prioritize breadth: 15 sources cited once each > 3 sources cited 5 times each
- Include foundational works AND recent research

---

## Evidence Integrity

**CRITICAL:** Only cite information from your provided sources.
- Include statistics and findings ONLY if they appear in the evidence
- If a source doesn't provide numbers, describe qualitatively
- Never fabricate statistics, percentages, or specific claims
- Write "found a positive relationship" rather than inventing "r = 0.45"
