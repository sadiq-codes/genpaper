# Research Article Context

A research article presents original analysis, interpretation, and argument based on systematic research. It can involve primary data collection OR secondary analysis of existing literature and data.

---

## Research Article Modes

Research articles operate in different modes depending on data source.

**How to identify the mode:** Look at the data/evidence source:
- You collected new data (surveys, interviews, experiments, observations) → Mode A
- You analyze existing literature, datasets, or texts to make an argument → Mode B
- You combine both → Mode C

### Mode A: Primary Empirical Research
You collected original data through surveys, experiments, interviews, observations, archival research, etc.
- Methodology describes YOUR data collection procedures
- Results present YOUR original findings (quantitative statistics OR qualitative themes)
- Structure options:
  - **IMRAD** (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) - common in sciences and social sciences
  - **Thematic** (Introduction, thematic sections, Conclusion) - common in humanities

### Mode B: Secondary Analysis / Synthesis Research
You analyze existing literature, published datasets, or documents to generate new insights.
- This is LEGITIMATE research - not "just" a literature review
- The contribution comes from novel analysis, synthesis, framework application, or argument
- The paper makes a CLAIM and supports it with evidence from existing sources

**Key difference from Literature Reviews:**
- Literature reviews survey a field to provide comprehensive background
- Secondary analysis papers make a specific ARGUMENT using existing evidence as support
- Structure serves the argument, not just source organization

### Mode C: Mixed Methods
Combines primary data with secondary analysis of existing literature.

## Introduction Structure (CARS Framework)

Research article introductions follow the **CARS model** (Create A Research Space):

### Move 1: Establish Territory (Opening)
**Purpose:** Show the topic matters and capture reader interest

- **Hook:** Open with a compelling fact, significant problem, striking statistic, or provocative question
- **Importance:** Establish why this topic deserves attention (practical significance, theoretical importance, urgency)
- **Background:** Provide essential context the reader needs (not exhaustive - save details for lit review)
- **Scope:** Briefly indicate the breadth of existing work on this topic

**Signal phrases:** "X is a critical challenge in..." / "Recent developments in X have prompted..." / "The relationship between X and Y has significant implications for..."

### Move 2: Establish the Niche (Gap)
**Purpose:** Identify what's missing, contested, or problematic that justifies your research

- **Gap types:** Knowledge gap, methodological gap, theoretical gap, contextual gap, practical gap
- **Be specific:** Vague gaps ("more research is needed") lead to vague papers
- **Connect to Move 1:** The gap should arise naturally from the territory you established

**Signal phrases:** "However, what remains unclear is..." / "Despite extensive research, X has not been examined..." / "Existing studies have overlooked..." / "A critical limitation of prior work is..."

### Move 3: Occupy the Niche (Your Contribution)
**Purpose:** State what this paper does and preview its structure

- **Research question or thesis:** State your central question, hypothesis, or argument clearly
- **Approach:** Briefly indicate your method or analytical framework
- **Contribution:** What will readers gain? What's the value-add?
- **Paper map:** Preview the structure ("This paper first examines X, then analyzes Y, before concluding with Z")

**Signal phrases:** "This paper argues that..." / "We investigate whether..." / "This study addresses this gap by..." / "The analysis proceeds as follows..."

### Introduction Anti-Patterns
❌ Opening with dictionary definitions ("According to Merriam-Webster...")
❌ Overly broad openings ("Since the dawn of civilization..." / "Throughout human history...")
❌ Jumping straight to your thesis without establishing context
❌ Burying the research question at the end of a long literature dump
❌ Stating the gap without explaining why it matters
❌ Vague contributions ("This paper adds to the literature...")

## Argument Development

Research articles build arguments through logical progression, not just information presentation.

### Building Your Argument

1. **State your position clearly** - Academic writing requires taking a stance. Excessive hedging undermines your contribution.

2. **Evidence hierarchy:**
   - **Primary:** Direct empirical findings (from your data or cited studies)
   - **Secondary:** Theoretical reasoning supported by established frameworks
   - **Tertiary:** Logical inference from accepted premises

3. **Show reasoning, not just conclusions:**
   ❌ "The evidence supports X." (Why? How?)
   ✅ "The consistent positive correlations across diverse samples [1][2][3], combined with the plausible mechanism identified by [4], provide converging evidence for X."

4. **Build cumulatively:** Each paragraph should advance the argument, building on what came before.

### Handling Counterarguments and Alternative Views

Strong papers acknowledge and address opposing perspectives:

1. **Acknowledge:** Present the alternative view fairly
2. **Evaluate:** Assess its strengths and weaknesses
3. **Respond:** Explain why your position is stronger or how you reconcile the tension

**Signal phrases for counterargument:**
- "Critics of this view argue that..."
- "An alternative interpretation suggests..."
- "While this position has merit, it overlooks..."
- "This objection, however, can be addressed by..."

### Logical Flow Requirements

Each section should:
- Begin by connecting to what came before (backward-looking transition)
- Accomplish its specific purpose in the argument
- End by previewing or setting up what comes next (forward-looking transition)

## Section-by-Section Structure

### Introduction
See CARS Framework above. Target: 10-15% of paper length.

### Literature Review
**Purpose:** Establish theoretical foundation and show how your work fits in the scholarly conversation.

For Primary Research: Brief, focused review establishing the theoretical framework (NOT the main content).
For Secondary Analysis: More extensive engagement with the literature you're synthesizing.

**Requirements:**
- Synthesize sources (integrate, compare, contrast) - don't just list them
- Identify debates, contradictions, and patterns
- End with clear gap that your research addresses
- Cite at least 50% of available relevant sources

### Methodology
**For Quantitative Research:**
- Sample/participants (who, how many, how selected)
- Variables (independent, dependent, control)
- Data collection procedures
- Analytical methods with justification
- Validity and reliability measures

**For Qualitative Research:**
- Participants/sources and selection rationale
- Data collection (interviews, observations, documents, artifacts)
- Analytical approach (thematic analysis, grounded theory, discourse analysis, etc.)
- Trustworthiness measures (triangulation, member checking, audit trail)

**For Humanities/Interpretive Research:**
- Primary sources analyzed (texts, artworks, historical documents, etc.)
- Theoretical or interpretive framework
- Analytical approach and its justification
- Scope and selection criteria

**For Secondary Analysis:**
- Sources analyzed (what literature/data, why these sources)
- Analytical framework (how you approached the analysis)
- Procedures (how you identified patterns, coded themes, etc.)
- Rigor measures (how you ensured validity)

### Results / Findings
**For Quantitative Research:**
- Present YOUR data with appropriate statistics
- Use tables and figures
- Report results without interpretation
- Minimal to no citations (this is YOUR data)

**For Qualitative Research:**
- Present themes/categories with illustrative quotes
- Use data excerpts as evidence
- Balance description with initial interpretation
- Minimal citations (your analysis of your data)

**For Secondary Analysis:**
- Present your analytical findings (patterns, themes, arguments)
- Support claims with evidence from sources (citations expected)
- Organize by argument structure, not by source
- Tables can synthesize findings across sources

### Discussion
**Purpose:** Interpret your findings in context of existing knowledge.

- **Summarize key findings** (brief - don't just repeat Results)
- **Compare with prior work** (agreements, disagreements, extensions)
- **Explain mechanisms** (why did you find what you found?)
- **Discuss implications** (theoretical, practical, methodological)
- **Address limitations** honestly but without undermining your work

### Conclusion
See Conclusion Framework below.

## Conclusion Framework

The conclusion provides closure, significance, and direction.

### Required Elements

1. **Restate the core contribution** (1-2 sentences)
   - Don't copy your thesis - synthesize what the paper demonstrated
   - "This analysis revealed that..." / "This study demonstrated..."

2. **Summarize key findings** (2-3 sentences)
   - Hit the highlights, not every detail
   - Connect findings back to the research question

3. **Discuss implications** (2-4 sentences)
   - **Theoretical:** How does this change/advance understanding?
   - **Practical:** What should practitioners, policymakers, or stakeholders do?
   - **Methodological:** What does this suggest for future research approaches?

4. **Acknowledge limitations** (1-2 sentences)
   - Be honest but don't undermine your contribution
   - Frame as scope boundaries: "This study focused on X; future work should examine Y"

5. **Future directions** (1-2 sentences)
   - Be specific, not generic
   ❌ "More research is needed"
   ✅ "Future studies should examine whether these findings hold in [specific context]"

### Conclusion Anti-Patterns
❌ Introducing new arguments, evidence, or citations
❌ Opening with "In conclusion," "To summarize," "In summary"
❌ Simply restating the introduction verbatim
❌ Excessive hedging that undermines your contribution
❌ Generic future directions without specificity
❌ Overstating implications beyond what findings support

## When to Cite (Section-Specific)

### Introduction
Cite to establish the problem and context.
- **Cite:** Statistics showing problem importance, prior findings motivating your work, theoretical frameworks
- **No citation:** Your research questions, your paper's contribution statement

### Literature Review
Cite extensively - you're reporting what others found.
- **Cite:** Every finding, theory, or method from another author
- **No citation:** Your synthesis, your identification of gaps, your analytical observations

### Methodology
Cite sparingly - only for established methods.
- **Cite:** Validated instruments, established procedures, methodological frameworks
- **No citation:** Your specific procedures and decisions

### Results (Primary Research)
Minimal to no citations - these are YOUR findings.
- **No citation:** Your data, statistics, tables, findings
- **Rare citation:** Only if directly comparing a specific result to a prior finding

### Results/Findings (Secondary Analysis)
Moderate to heavy citations - you're analyzing existing evidence.
- **Cite:** All claims drawn from sources
- **No citation:** Your analytical observations integrating multiple sources

### Discussion
Moderate citations - interpreting findings in context.
- **Cite:** When comparing to prior work, when invoking theories
- **No citation:** Your interpretations, implications, conclusions

### Conclusion
Minimal to no citations.
- **Cite:** Only if referencing specific literature for future directions
- **No citation:** Your summary, implications, limitations assessment

## Language Guidelines

### Appropriate Research Article Language:

**Quantitative/Scientific:**
- "Our findings indicate..." / "The results demonstrate..."
- "We found that X was significantly related to Y (β = value, p < value)"
- "Hypothesis 1 was supported: [specific finding]"

**Qualitative/Social Science:**
- "Participants described..." / "A recurring theme was..."
- "The data revealed three primary categories..."
- "This pattern suggests..."

**Humanities/Interpretive:**
- "This analysis reveals that..." / "A close reading demonstrates..."
- "The text suggests..." / "This evidence supports the argument that..."
- "The author's use of X serves to..."

**All Disciplines:**
- "The evidence suggests..." (when synthesizing multiple sources)
- "This finding aligns with / contradicts prior work..."

### Avoid (Across All Disciplines):
- "Studies have shown..." (too vague - which studies? cite them)
- "According to Smith (2020)..." as paragraph opener (integrate sources)
- "The literature suggests..." (vague - be specific)
- "Scholars have found..." (attribute specifically)
- "It is interesting to note..." (everything should be interesting - just state it)

## Quality Criteria

Research articles must demonstrate:

1. **Clear problem formulation** - Specific research question or thesis, not vague exploration
2. **Logical argument structure** - Claims supported by evidence with clear reasoning
3. **Methodological rigor** - Appropriate methods with transparency about limitations
4. **Evidence-based claims** - Every assertion supported by data or citations
5. **Critical engagement** - Address counterarguments and alternative interpretations
6. **Original contribution** - Clear statement of what this paper adds to knowledge
7. **Appropriate scope** - Conclusions proportionate to evidence presented

---

## Evidence Integrity

**CRITICAL: Only cite what is in your sources**

- Include statistics only if they appear in your evidence
- If a source lacks specific numbers, describe qualitatively
- Never fabricate citations, statistics, or claims
- Better to write "showed a positive relationship" than invent "r = 0.45"

For illustrative/template papers without real data:
- Clearly state in Limitations that data is illustrative
- Make illustrative data realistic and internally consistent
- Never present illustrative data as actual research findings
